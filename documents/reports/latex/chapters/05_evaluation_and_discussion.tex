This chapter reports what the measurements showed. It keeps four questions apart, because they are different questions: how good the dataset turned out to be, whether the laptop could do the job, how the two models scored on the validation set, and how they scored on the 220 messages that were set aside before training and kept unseen until the end.

Keeping those four questions apart matters, because a high validation score does not prove the dataset is realistic, a hardware measurement says nothing about accuracy, and a single run on the set-aside messages cannot show how much the number would move if everything were run again.

\section{Quality of the Dataset}

The final set holds 2{,}097 messages: 1{,}658 for training, 219 for validation, and 220 set aside. Table~\ref{tab:dataset-stats} gives the counts per class.

Two rules were fixed before the split was made, and both held. The first is that every message written from one source article stays in the same part, so no article has some of its messages used for training and others kept for testing. The second is that no single article may supply more than 8\% of the whole set, so one article cannot dominate a class; the largest one ended at 7.9638\%.

The judge model scored every message. Judging was not done in one sitting: an earlier pass had already scored most of the set, and when the corpus was finalised only the messages whose text had changed since needed scoring again. That is why 1{,}561 messages kept the score they already had and 536 were scored fresh, giving 2{,}097 in total. Nothing was skipped.

In total 1{,}395 passed, which is 66.52\%. The judge is asked to score five things and to mark a message as passing only if all five score at least 3 out of 5; the exact wording it is given is in Appendix~6.2. Figure~\ref{fig:corpus-quality} gives the average for each of the five and how many messages passed in each class.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{figures/corpus_judge_quality.png}
\caption{What the judge model found. Left: the average score on each of the five checks, out of 5. Right: how many messages in each class passed all five.}
\label{fig:corpus-quality}
\end{figure}

The two lowest averages are for whether a message reads like a real scam and whether its risk level fits. Those are the two hardest things to get right when a model is writing the message instead of a person.

The per-class figures need reading with care. Zalo passes at 99.7\% and task scam at 47.5\%, and that gap is not evidence that the Zalo messages are better. The Zalo messages are the ones that were rewritten, and they were rewritten by a model from the same family as the judge that later scored them, although under a different prompt and a separate task. A checker marking work from its own family is not an independent opinion, so the safest reading is that the Zalo figure measures agreement between two related models rather than quality. The task-scam figure, where writer and judge are genuinely different, is the more honest one to quote.

A two-thirds pass rate needs reading correctly. The judge is strict: one weak score out of five fails the whole message. The messages that failed were not silently kept. They are the reason the repair work described in Chapter~IV exists.

The manual review covered 100 messages, drawn evenly across all four classes and across both judge outcomes. The reviewer passed 44 and failed 56, and agreed with the judge on 87 of the 100. That 44 out of 100 is a result about this mixed sample. It is not a random sample of the whole set, and it does not mean 56\% of the dataset is bad.

The label review covered all 324 messages the judge had flagged. The reviewer dropped 91 and agreed that 233 needed a different label: 48 belonged to bank impersonation, 177 to Zalo social engineering, and 8 to benign. Only 57 of those corrections were added, because the other 176 all traced back to a single source article and would have made the Zalo class look more varied than it really is, when in fact those messages are near-copies of one another.

\section{Hardware Measurement Results}

The two short runs answered one question, which was whether this laptop could train either way. They say nothing about accuracy, and no model from either run was kept.

Ordinary LoRA left 9~MiB of video memory free at its tightest and pointed at roughly eighteen hours of computation. QLoRA peaked at 7{,}516 of 8{,}151~MiB, leaving a workable margin, at a median of 3.462389 seconds per step. Neither produced an out-of-memory error. Figure~\ref{fig:gpu-telemetry} shows the card under load during the QLoRA run.

The conclusion is narrow: LoRA was runnable but left no room for anything to go wrong across an eighteen-hour run, while QLoRA had margin to spare. No full LoRA model exists, so there is no accuracy figure to compare, and no speed comparison is drawn between Qwen and PhoBERT.

\section{Validation Results}

Both models were trained with the same seed and selected on the same 219 validation messages. Qwen's best checkpoint came at step 200 and PhoBERT's at step 100. Neither produced an unreadable output, and neither called a dangerous message safe.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering

\caption{Both models on the same 219 validation messages. Left: the two overall scores. Right: how many of the risky messages in each class were caught. The vertical axis starts at 0.90, because every value falls between 0.95 and 1.00 and a full scale would render them as identical bars.}
\label{fig:validation-comparison}
\end{figure}

\noindent Qwen scored slightly higher on both overall measures. The per-class panel matters more: PhoBERT caught every bank-impersonation message and missed some Zalo messages, while Qwen did the reverse. Neither is uniformly better, and each model was trained and evaluated once, so these numbers justify accepting the two selected checkpoints and nothing more.

\section{Final Evaluation Results}

These 220 messages were separated from the rest when the dataset was first split, and neither model, nor any decision taken during the project, saw them until everything else was finished. Both finished models were then run over them exactly once. Figure~\ref{fig:terminal-comparison} gives the results.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering

\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:terminal-confusion} shows where each model went wrong. Rows are the true label and columns are what the model predicted.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{figures/terminal_evaluation_confusion.png}
\caption{Where each model made its mistakes. Shaded cells off the diagonal are errors: four for Qwen and two for PhoBERT.}
\label{fig:terminal-confusion}
\end{figure}

PhoBERT came out higher on accuracy and on both F1 measures. Qwen came out higher on the benign class, where it raised no false alarms at all while PhoBERT raised one.

The most important column is the last one. A message that is dangerous but gets called safe is the expensive mistake, because the user is told there is nothing to worry about. Both models made exactly one such mistake, so on the measure that matters most for this system they tied.

Each model was trained once, so that difference is a result about this one run and not a measured limit of either model. It is not enough to conclude that PhoBERT classifies better in general. It is also not a reason to replace Qwen, because PhoBERT returns a label and nothing else: it cannot produce the risk tier, the quoted evidence, or the written explanation that the system exists to give.

\section{Limits of the Evidence}
\label{sec:evidence-limits}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The messages are generated, not collected.} They were written from real public scam warnings rather than taken from real victims. Careful generation, repair, and checking improve the quality of the set, but they cannot show that it matches real scam traffic \cite{kaddour2023synthetic}.

\item \textbf{The manual checking covers a sample.} 100 messages reviewed by hand and 324 labels corrected is real work, but it is not the whole dataset. The 44-out-of-100 pass rate is a warning against reading the judge's high averages as proof that every message sounds natural.

\item \textbf{The judge is not fully independent.} The rewritten Zalo messages were written by a model from the same family as the judge that scored them, although under a different prompt and a separate task, and they pass at 99.7\% against 47.5\% for task scam. For those messages the judge is checking work related to its own, so its verdict is not a second opinion.

\item \textbf{Everything was run once.} Each model was trained one time with one seed. There is no repeat run to compare against, so there is no way to say how much these numbers would move, and no basis for calling one model reliably better.

\item \textbf{The held-out messages were used once and not reused.} The result was not used to retrain, to repair data, to move a threshold, or to pick a different checkpoint.

\item \textbf{No deployment version was built.} The exported model runs locally, but no version was fitted on all the data afterwards, and no operating threshold was calibrated.

\item \textbf{PhoBERT was not exported to GGUF.} It was kept as a standard Transformers model. GGUF conversion exists to make a large generative model runnable on an ordinary CPU \cite{kristiani2026deploying}, and PhoBERT is a small encoder that already runs on a CPU without it. It is also the comparison baseline rather than the model the system ships, so converting it would not have changed any result reported here.

\item \textbf{Genuine messages that mention logging in are over-flagged.} Six ordinary bank messages were put through the finished model. Four were returned benign; the two that were flagged are the only two that ask the reader to log in, one of them a real one-time-password notice (Section~\ref{sec:false-alarm}). Almost every scam in the training data works by getting someone to log in or hand over a code, so the model has learned to treat that language as the signal itself. These are false alarms rather than missed scams and the advice given with them was still safe, but the behaviour is systematic, and it shows that the scores above were measured on generated messages and do not transfer automatically to writing from outside that set.

\item \textbf{Text only.} The system reads pasted text. Screenshot and QR-code reading do not exist, and the system has never been tested against real scam messages arriving on a real phone.
\end{enumerate}

Within those limits, the project does show a complete chain: a dataset built so that reworded messages cannot leak between splits, hardware measured before committing to a training method, two models trained on the same laptop, training graphs drawn from the run logs, a working exported model, and one evaluation on messages neither model had seen.
