The following sections review the existing work that shaped this system: how Vietnamese phishing looks in practice, what other people have tried with language models, and why no ready-made dataset was usable here.

\section{Vietnamese Financial Phishing}

Vietnamese users meet financial phishing through SMS, Zalo, and other social platforms. Group-IB documented a campaign that targeted customers of Vietnamese banks with cloned login pages sent by SMS \cite{groupib2022laser}, and the Vietnamese authority for information security warned about bank-impersonation messages that exploited the new biometric-authentication rules \cite{ais2024biometricwarning}. These are documented examples, and they are what the four threat classes in this project are based on. They are not a claim about how common phishing is nationally.

Vietnamese phishing text is hard for NLP in specific ways. Diacritics change meaning --- \textit{ma}, \textit{m\~a}, and \textit{m\`a} are three different words --- and informal messages often drop or misplace them. In SMS in particular they are frequently left off altogether, so the same word can appear in several forms across the corpus. Le-Hong~\cite{lehong2021diacritics} showed that restoring diacritics with deep learning improves later classification accuracy on social-media text. Early work by Pham and Le-Hong~\cite{pham2016vietnamese} filtered Vietnamese spam SMS by content, but it could only look at which words appeared in a message, not at how they were used. PhoBERT~\cite{nguyen2020phobert}, the first large Vietnamese-only language model, reached strong results on several Vietnamese tasks including text classification, and became a standard starting point for Vietnamese work. ViSoBERT~\cite{nguyen2023visobert} extended that to social-media text and beat PhoBERT on informal Vietnamese tasks such as spam and hate-speech detection, which is close to the register of phishing messages. Tran et al.~\cite{tran2022phobertcnn} put a Text-CNN head on PhoBERT for Vietnamese hate and offensive-content detection and reported the same informal-writing problems that appear in this project's messages: abbreviations, mixed Vietnamese and English, and inconsistent diacritics. Their work also shows that a PhoBERT classification head is a workable production pattern for short Vietnamese text. The same alternative is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:qwen-vs-phobert}.

Across the Vietnamese NLP and phishing resources reviewed for this thesis, no public benchmark was found for Vietnamese financial phishing classification with grounded explanations. The closest available resources are general Vietnamese spam corpora, which do not separate threat types such as bank impersonation, task scam, and social engineering, and do not mark the evidence inside each message. Nguyen et al.~\cite{nguyen2025fakeads} is the nearest Vietnamese fraud-detection system found: a pipeline for spotting fake property advertisements, reaching 91.5\% accuracy. It targets a different kind of fraud and a different kind of input, but it confirms that Vietnamese fraud detection is an active area with nothing published yet for financial phishing specifically. This review does not prove that no such dataset exists anywhere. It does mean the available ones were not good enough to build on, so two of them were checked directly instead of taken on trust.

The first check was the size of the source itself. The live scam-warning listing on \textit{tinnhiemmang.vn} was paged through to the end and holds 74 advisory articles in total, of which this project already uses 67. There is no large untapped supply of real cases at that source. It publishes advisory write-ups about scams rather than the scam messages themselves, so generating messages from those articles was necessary to reach a useful training size, not a shortcut.

The second check was a candidate dataset that was tested rather than trusted. A Vietnamese SMS dataset published on Hugging Face under a CC~BY~4.0 licence~\cite{trannguyen2026vnsms} declares 2{,}991 messages with a ready-made train/test split. Comparing the two halves directly, by exact text match after normalising Unicode, case, and spacing, showed that 25.3\% of the phishing messages in its test half already appear word for word in its training half. Any model evaluated on that split would therefore score higher than it deserves, because part of the test is material it has already seen. The dataset's documentation was also edited four times within three days of upload while the data files themselves stayed the same. Because that overlap is confirmed, the dataset was not used here. Making it usable would mean re-labelling it, re-splitting it, and checking where the messages came from, at which point building the dataset from scratch becomes the more viable job to perform.

\section{LLM-based Phishing and Fraud Detection}

Several groups have applied large language models to phishing detection. Patel et al.~\cite{patel2024evaluating} tested 15 language models on phishing-email identification using 419-scam datasets and found GPT-3.5 variants the most effective, while noting that every model struggled with unfamiliar phishing wording it had not seen during pre-training. Koide et al.~\cite{koide2024chatspamdetector} built ChatSpamDetector, reaching 99.70\% accuracy with GPT-4 while also writing a natural-language explanation for each decision. The aim here is close to that, except that their approach depends on sending the message to a cloud API.

A question that matters directly here is whether fine-tuning beats prompting. Trad and Chehab~\cite{trad2024prompt} compared prompt engineering against fine-tuning for phishing-URL detection and found the fine-tuned smaller model better (97.29\% F1) than the prompted larger one (92.74\% F1), which supports fine-tuning a 4B model rather than prompting something bigger. Goldenits et al.~\cite{goldenits2026small} evaluated 15 small language models, from 1B to 70B parameters, for detecting phishing websites locally, and showed that models running on the user's own machine can replace cloud APIs without giving up privacy, which is the direction taken here.

On explanations, Uddin and Sarker~\cite{uddin2024explainable} fine-tuned DistilBERT for phishing-email classification and attached LIME explanations to its decisions, showing that a transformer classifier can be made to say why it decided what it decided. Xue et al.~\cite{xue2025multiphishguard} built MultiPhishGuard, in which several language models work together and are trained against each other: one keeps trying to slip phishing past the others, which forces the detector to improve and to explain more of what it found. Both support pairing a classification with a structured explanation, although neither works on Vietnamese and neither runs only on the user machine.

Closest to this project, Hossain et al.~\cite{hossain2025scambaiting} built a real-time scam-detection system that keeps the conversation spread across the users' own devices instead of gathering it on one central server, for the same privacy reason that drives the local-only boundary here (Section~\ref{sec:local-privacy}). Their system follows a live conversation rather than classifying one message with quoted evidence, so the problem is framed differently, but their reason for keeping the data on the device is the same as the reason here: privacy is part of the design, not a feature added at the end.

\section{Local Inference as a Privacy Control}
\label{sec:local-privacy}

A suspicious financial message usually contains the very things a person should not hand over: names, phone numbers, OTP codes, and account details. The NIST Privacy Framework argues for finding and reducing privacy risks wherever data is processed \cite{nist2026privacyframework}, and this project applies that idea in the simplest possible way, by never letting the message leave the device.

The risk with cloud tools is not theoretical. In March 2023 OpenAI disclosed a caching bug that exposed the chat history and payment details of roughly 1.2\% of ChatGPT Plus subscribers \cite{openai2023breach}. In the same year Samsung engineers pasted confidential chip design code into ChatGPT, and the company banned generative AI tools outright \cite{bloomberg2023samsung}. Both cases involve exactly the pattern this project avoids: sensitive text sent to somebody else's server. Table~\ref{tab:cloud-api-incidents} sets out what was exposed in each.

Cloud providers do offer privacy settings, and what those settings actually promise needs stating precisely. OpenAI's Temporary Chat keeps a conversation out of the chat history, does not use it to improve the model, and does not write anything into memory. What it does not do is stop the message being sent. OpenAI's own documentation states that a copy may still be kept for up to 30 days for safety purposes \cite{openai2026temporarychat}. Under the strongest privacy setting the provider offers, a message carrying a bank account number and a one-time password therefore still leaves the device and sits on someone else's server for up to a month.

The 2023 incident makes the same point from the other direction. That exposure was not caused by a user choosing the wrong setting; it was caused by a bug on the provider's side \cite{openai2023breach}. A privacy setting can only be as good as the system enforcing it. Running the model on the device removes the question rather than answering it.

Running a language model on the user's own machine is already practical. Google shipped a 4~GB Gemini Nano model inside Chrome for local summarisation and scam detection, with nothing sent to a remote server \cite{cyberinsider2026geminanano}. Kristiani et al.~\cite{kristiani2026deploying} reviewed the main ways of shrinking a model to fit a small device --- cutting away parts that contribute little, and storing the remaining numbers at lower precision --- and identified GGUF as one workable format for machines with no GPU at all. Their survey covers edge devices generally, not just laptops, which is the direction this kind of tool is moving: the same compressed-model techniques are what let a language model run on a phone or a handheld translation device rather than in a data centre. Maiya~\cite{maiya2025onprem} released OnPrem.LLM, a toolkit for running language models over sensitive documents entirely offline, built on llama.cpp, which is the same runtime used here.

How aggressively the model is compressed matters, because compressing further saves space but costs accuracy. Kurt~\cite{kurt2026quantization} tested every llama.cpp compression format and found that Q8\_0 keeps almost all of the original quality while still shrinking the file enough for ordinary hardware. Q8\_0 was chosen for the exported model here for that reason.

\section{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning}

Fully retraining a model with billions of parameters needs hardware that most people cannot afford. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning avoids that by leaving almost all of the model alone and training only a small extra piece. Houlsby et al.~\cite{houlsby2019parameter} introduced the idea with adapter modules, which came within 0.4\% of the accuracy of full retraining while adding only 3.6\% more parameters. Hu et al.~\cite{hu2022lora} proposed LoRA, which stores the update as two small matrices instead of a full-size copy of the model's weights, so there is no need to keep a second copy of everything in memory. Dettmers et al.~\cite{dettmers2023qlora} then combined that with 4-bit storage of the frozen original model, producing QLoRA and making fine-tuning possible on a single consumer GPU with as little as 6~GB of video memory.

Han et al.~\cite{han2024peft} surveyed the family of these methods. Nwaiwu~\cite{nwaiwu2025peft} compared three of them on text classification with little training data and found LoRA the most accurate, at 0.909 F1, while a lighter method reached 98\% of that score using 3\% of the parameters. QLoRA was chosen for this project because it keeps LoRA's accuracy while cutting the memory enough to train on a laptop.

An early check in this project measured how much the fine-tuning is worth. The same base model, Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, was scored on the same 254 messages twice: once with prompting alone and no fine-tuning, and once after QLoRA. Prompting alone reached a macro F1 of 0.6702; after fine-tuning the same model reached 0.9625 on those messages. That was an early measurement on a smaller validation set than the one used later, and it is not one of this thesis's results, but it is the project's own reason for fine-tuning at all rather than simply prompting a general model.

\section{Generative Classification with Decoder-Only Models}

Once a model has been fine-tuned, something still has to turn its output into an actual class decision, and there are two normal ways to do that.

The first is to bolt a small classifier onto the model's internal representation and train that piece directly. This is the older and more common approach, and it is what PhoBERT does here (Section~\ref{sec:qwen-vs-phobert}).

The second is to make the model simply write the class name as text. Raffel et al.~\cite{raffel2020t5} showed with T5 that one text-in, text-out model can handle classification, translation, and summarisation at once, with the class name written out as ordinary text. The alternative is a fixed set of output positions, one per class, where the model produces a score for each and the highest one wins; a model built that way can only ever choose between the classes it was wired for, and cannot say anything else. Schick and Sch{\"u}tze~\cite{schick2021pet} named the mechanism a \textit{verbalizer}: a fixed list of exact strings, one per class, that the model is trained to reproduce. Narang et al.~\cite{narang2020wt5} extended it with WT5 so the model writes the label and the explanation together, and Yousefiramandi and Cooney~\cite{yousefiramandi2025finetuning} compared the two routes head to head on the same base model. Which of them this project uses, and why, is in Section~\ref{sec:qwen-vs-phobert}.

\section{Synthetic Data Generation for NLP}

No public Vietnamese financial phishing dataset was found that has both threat-type labels and marked evidence inside each message. The training data was therefore generated from the real scam-warning material. One model wrote the first pass, a large part of it turned out not to be usable and was rewritten, a stronger model then scored every message, and a Vietnamese-speaking reviewer checked a sample by hand. How that was done is in Chapter~IV and what it produced is in Chapter~V.

The direction of that arrangement is deliberate. Writing thousands of messages is the bulk of the work, so a cheaper model does it; checking them is the part that has to be trusted, so a stronger model does that. Reversing the two would be the weaker design: a checker less capable than the writer tends to approve what it cannot properly analyse, and the check stops meaning very much. In this direction a message only survives if a model at least as capable as the one that wrote it could not find a fault in it. Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2023judging} found that a strong model's judgements agree with human preferences at roughly the rate two people agree with each other, which is why the checking side justifies the stronger model. They also documented how such judges fail, including a tendency to favour text in their own style, which is precisely why the 100-message human review exists here: to check the judge rather than to take it on trust.

Recent work supports generating data this way when real data is scarce. Kaddour and Liu~\cite{kaddour2023synthetic} showed that text generated by a teacher model gives real accuracy gains for classification when training data is limited. Long et al.~\cite{long2024synthetic} surveyed the area and found that showing the generator a few real examples first works better than asking it to invent messages from nothing, which is exactly the seed-based design used here. Li et al.~\cite{li2024datageneration} studied how the wording of the request and the variety of the output affect quality, and Anikina et al.~\cite{anikina2025rigorous} tested generation across 11 different languages and found that giving examples in the target language closes the gap to real data to within 5\%, which supports building the messages from the Vietnamese scam-warning articles collected for this project rather than from English examples. Those articles are the \textit{seeds}: each one describes a real scam in general terms, and the messages in the dataset are written from it (Section~\ref{sec:quality-check}).

The usual problems with generated data are managed by the checks described in Chapter~III.

\section{Explainable AI for Cybersecurity}

The system is designed to explain itself from the start, rather than having an explanation bolted on after the decision is already made. It returns a risk tier, threat labels, and the specific phrases in the message that triggered the decision, instead of a vague confidence number the user cannot check. Rjoub et al.~\cite{rjoub2023surveyxai} reviewed explanation techniques in cybersecurity and identified the gap between a model being accurate and a user trusting it. Sharma et al.~\cite{sharma2025comprehensive} sorted the main explanation methods used in security into groups and noted that grounding an explanation in the specific domain remains underexplored.

Lim et al.~\cite{lim2025explicate} built EXPLICATE, which ties phishing explanations to evidence in the text and showed that users trusted it more than a system that only reported a confidence score. Their finding that evidence-based explanations make people more likely to follow the security advice is the reason this project returns quoted cues next to the risk tier.

\section{What Is Still Missing}

Taken together, the work reviewed above has made real progress on detecting phishing with language models, on fine-tuning them cheaply, and on making them explain themselves. What no single reviewed system does is all of it at once. None of them combines a Vietnamese phishing dataset with proper threat-type labels, a fine-tuned model that runs locally on ordinary hardware with no cloud service, explanations grounded in the actual words of the message, and an evaluation that treats a missed scam as the more serious error, because it is. That combination is the gap this thesis works on.
